# PNG to SVG: What Vectorising Can and Cannot Do (Free, 2026)

Converting a PNG to SVG sounds like changing a file extension. It is not — it is a change of representation. A PNG stores a grid of pixels; an SVG stores shapes and paths. Producing one from the other means **tracing**, and tracing works brilliantly on some images and poorly on others.

Knowing which category your image falls into saves a lot of disappointment. The [PNG to SVG converter](/tools/png-to-svg) uses edge detection and path tracing, and runs entirely in your browser.

## What tracing actually does

The converter looks for edges — boundaries where colour changes sharply — and fits paths along them. Those paths are then filled with the colours found on either side, producing an SVG that renders as flat regions rather than pixels.

Two consequences follow immediately:

**It produces shapes, not samples.** The output is a set of filled regions approximating your image. On a logo with clean edges that is effectively a perfect reproduction. On a photograph, where colour shifts gradually across nearly every pixel, there are almost no true edges to find, and the result becomes a posterised mosaic of blobs.

**It cannot invent detail that is not there.** Faint anti-aliasing, soft shadows and subtle gradients either get traced as many thin bands or get simplified away. Both are visible.

## Which images vectorise well

| Image type | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Logos and wordmarks | Excellent | Flat colours, hard edges, few distinct regions |
| Icons and UI glyphs | Excellent | Simple geometry, solid fills, transparent background |
| Line art and technical drawings | Very good | The subject *is* lines, which is what tracing looks for |
| Charts and diagrams | Good | Flat fills with clean boundaries |
| Text-heavy graphics (if not available as text) | Fair | Edges trace, but small type becomes lumpy |
| Photographs | Poor | No real edges; everything becomes banded regions |
| Images with gradients and soft shadows | Poor | Gradients are the opposite of what tracing wants |

The dividing line is simple: **if a human would describe the image as having "shapes", it traces well. If they would describe it as having "tones", it does not.**

## Why photographs never work

This is worth stating plainly because it is the most common disappointment. A photograph contains very few true edges. A face in a portrait, for instance, has continuous variation in tone across the skin — there is no line where "face" changes to "cheek". A tracer sees a smooth gradient and must choose thresholds, producing concentric bands that look like a contour map.

For photographs, the right tools are format conversion and compression, not vectorisation: [convert the format](/tools/image-convert) or [compress the file](/tools/image-compress). Vectorising a photo is the wrong operation for the material.

## Why SVG is worth having anyway

Where it does apply, SVG brings real advantages:

- **Scales without loss.** One SVG renders crisply at favicon size and on a billboard — no separate exports at multiple resolutions.
- **Smaller files.** For flat graphics, an SVG is usually far smaller than a high-resolution PNG of the same artwork.
- **Editable.** Colours and paths can be changed in code or a vector editor, without re-exporting from the original design.
- **Retina-ready automatically.** No 2× or 3× asset management.

This is why logos and icons are almost always delivered as SVG on the web, even when the source artwork started as a raster export. The format itself is [an open W3C standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SVG), with implementation details documented on [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG).

## Getting a usable result

A few practical steps improve the output considerably:

1. **Start from the largest, cleanest source you have.** Tracing a small, blurry PNG produces small, blurry paths.
2. **Remove the background first if it is not part of the design.** A white rectangle behind a logo becomes a traced rectangle; a transparent background produces cleaner paths. A [background remover](/tools/remove-bg) handles this before you convert.
3. **Check the trace at display size, not zoomed in.** Traced paths have visible imperfections at high magnification that are irrelevant at the size you will actually use.
4. **Compare the file size.** If the SVG is much larger than the PNG, the image was probably not a good tracing candidate.
5. **Keep the original.** Tracing is a one-way transformation; nothing is gained by discarding the source.

## Converting the other way

SVG to PNG is the reverse operation and is occasionally necessary — some platforms, email clients and print workflows only accept raster images. Here the trade-off is resolution: choose the pixel dimensions you need, and remember that a PNG cannot be scaled back up afterwards without becoming soft. Convert at the largest size you might need, then [resize down](/tools/image-resize) for specific uses.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the SVG be identical to my PNG?**
No. Tracing produces an approximation built from filled paths. For a clean logo it can be visually indistinguishable at normal sizes; for anything with gradients or photographic content it will visibly differ. Expect a faithful silhouette, not a pixel-perfect copy.

**What is PNG to SVG actually good for?**
Logos, icons and line art — graphics made of flat shapes. That is where the technique shines and where SVG's benefits (scaling, file size, editability) matter most.

**Can I vectorise a photo?**
You can run it, but the result is not useful. Photographs lack the sharp edges tracing depends on, so the output becomes banded regions rather than a recognisable improvement. For photos, use format conversion and compression instead.

**Why is my SVG larger than the PNG?**
It means the image had too much tonal variation to trace efficiently — every region becomes a path, and thousands of tiny paths cost more than the pixels they replace. This is a signal that the image is not a good vectorisation candidate.

**Is my image uploaded?**
No. Tracing and conversion happen in your browser, so the file never leaves your device.
